0. Overview: a journey replayed every 20 milliseconds

The first idea to get straight: nowhere in the program does anything say "turn servo X by so many degrees" in order to walk. The upper layers only ever answer one question — "right now, where should the six feet be in the body frame". Then a fixed pipeline converts 6 positions into 18 angles and then into 18 pulse widths and sends them out. This pipeline runs 50 times a second (one frame every 20 ms); walking is this 50-frames-per-second "stream of position snapshots", and the servo end has no concept of "taking a step" at all.

1. Hardware: the three joints of one leg (turn them yourself)

Each leg has 3 servos and 3 links; from the body outward:

JointCommon nameAxis directionWhat it doesCode symbolLink length
coxaHipVertical (perpendicular to the adhesion surface)Sweeps the whole leg fore and aft in the horizontal planeγ (gamma), 0 = the leg's neutral direction43 mm
femurThighHorizontalRaises/lowers the leg in the vertical planeα (alpha), horizontal = 0, positive upward81 mm
tibiaShin/kneeHorizontalKnee flexion/extension, sets how far the foot is from the hipθ (theta) = angle between thigh and shin, straight = 180°123.7 mm

Intuitively: coxa handles fore/aft, femur handles height, femur+tibia together handle reach. While walking the foot must move in a straight line at constant speed, yet all three joints rotate — so the three angles must all change along curves at once, cooperating to "rotate out a straight line". That is exactly why it is recomputed every frame (the inverse kinematics of Section 5).

Leg numbering and orientation: left side front to back L1/L2/L3, right side R1/R2/R3. Each leg has a "neutral direction" (set by assembly, e.g. L1 points front-outward at 55°, L2 straight left at 90°), and γ=0 points along it. In all the "leg frame" calculations below, the x axis runs along each leg's own neutral direction — so all six legs can share the same IK formulas.

2. The essence of walking: stance phase and swing phase

The six legs take turns in two roles: stance phase (attached, acting as "ground", carrying the body across) and swing phase (released, going forward to find a new foothold). Understanding walking takes three steps: first agree on a viewpoint (2.1), then see how the stance legs carry the body (2.2), and finally how the schedule is laid out (2.3). The details of the foot-change motion itself are left to Chapter 3.

2.1 Agree on a viewpoint first, or nothing makes sense

  • Wall frame (reality): the attached feet are nailed to the wall and do not move a millimeter; the stance legs' joints turn slowly and the body is translated across. The feet do not push the wall; the body climbs over the feet.
  • Body frame (code): the body is always at the origin. "The body advances at 13 mm/s" becomes, in the books, "the target point of every stance foot moves backward at 13 mm/s".

These are the same thing, differing only by a frame. The test of whether you have got it is whether you can accept this sentence: "The stance foot's target moves every frame, yet the foot itself has not moved a millimeter on the wall." The part the target moved is exactly what became the body's advance.

Why does the code keep its books in the body frame? Because the next stage, IK, answers exactly "where is the foot relative to the body" (Section 5) — body-frame books go straight into IK, whereas the wall frame would need one extra conversion per frame. The price is that on first reading "the stance feet are moving backward" is confusing: what moves backward is the books, not the feet on the wall.

2.2 Stance phase: the body is not "pushed" forward

When five legs are attached, their 15 servos are not idle — every frame they turn slowly and in coordination to the angles IK recomputes, translating the body over the feet at constant speed. No pushing, no moment of force; a purely position-controlled geometric transport. Feel the numbers: at full speed, 13.3 mm/s, the body moves only 0.27 mm per frame (20 ms) — to the eye it is continuous sliding, to the servos a series of tiny position corrections.

Seen alone, the life of one leg is like a slider on a short rail (stride 40 mm as an example):

Foot-change landing point: ahead of the stance position by +20 mm (half stride)
   ↓ attached. The body advances; in the body frame the foot "slides" backward at constant speed
past the stance position 0
   ↓ keeps sliding
reaches about −20 mm behind the stance position ← exactly its foot-change window: lift, land again at +20 ahead, repeat

There is one more hard constraint that explains many seemingly roundabout designs in the engine: an attached foot must never slip on the wall (slip = the seal rubbed open = fall risk). So the stance target must be strictly complementary to the body motion and continuously integrated — a sudden change of velocity command must not make the target position jump; and every "pause" (clock stop, leak rescue, freeze) is implemented by the same action: freezing the stance targets in place.

2.3 The schedule: a gait = who changes feet when

Three terms used everywhere below: period T (climbing 3.6 s), duty (the fraction of the period spent in stance, climbing 5/6), window (the foot-change slot each leg gets within the period, T×(1−duty)=0.6 s). Draw the six legs' windows on one chart and the word "gait" becomes concrete — it is just a schedule:

  • Why climbing moves only one leg at a time: 5 cups carrying the whole robot is the safety floor. Two more gates at the window start: interlock (the other 5 feet must all be ATTACHED) and the lift threshold (all stance cups deeper than −50 kPa — a freshly attached neighbor is a "soft shoulder", lifting the next leg immediately would crush it); if not met, wait at the window start.
  • Why the order is R3→L1→R2→L3→R1→L2: consecutive lifts are always diagonal/crossed, spreading the disturbance across both sides. The old order, three left then three right, concentrated half a period of disturbance on one side — the structural root of the whole-robot sag on R2/R3 lifts measured on 08-19.
  • Clock time vs. real time: the body advances only while the clock runs. Each window = clock runs 0.6 s, then stops ≈3.5 s waiting for the changing leg to finish. The real machine's "walk a bit, pause, walk a bit" is the direct result of this schedule plus the clock-stop rules, not stuttering.

3. Climbing swing phase: the six-state machine of one foot change

On the floor the swing is just a sine arc "lift, move forward, put down". On the wall that does not work: releasing and sealing a cup are physical processes, and slapping down, dragging, or ripping off with vacuum still on all go wrong. So climb.py splits the swing into a state machine, each state doing exactly one thing:

StateWhat it doesWhy this state must exist (all lessons from real climbs)
VENT 0.3sStay in place, open the vent valve onlyLifting while venting: exhaust builds slower than lift-off, and the leg gets "yanked up" by residual vacuum (real machine, 08-19)
LIFT ≈0.83sPull out from the press position −18 along the wall normal and retreat to +15 above the surface, 33 mm total (40 mm/s)The cup itself rebounds 11–13 mm; retreat too little and the foot still rubs the wall. The phase clock reaches the window end and stops 0.3 s into LIFT (the foot is still 6 mm below the surface then) — the stance legs freeze earlier than the swing foot leaves the surface
TRANSFER 0.6sTranslate to directly above the landing point (smoothstep)Get "directly above" first, then descend, so the landing has no lateral velocity and does not drag the foot
DESCENDSlow vertical probe (30 mm/s), XY frozenKills "slapping" — the impact velocity at contact would break the seal
PRESSKeep pressing in 18 mm (15 mm/s)The bellows cup must be flattened to seal; 13 mm is mostly eaten by leg-chain give, measured deepened to 18
WAITPump, wait for the cup pressure past −30 kPa to confirm attachmentIf not attached, lift 5 mm and retry 5 mm deeper than last time (re-pressing at the same depth fails the same way); after 3 failures the whole robot freezes and alarms
The phase clock pauses at these points (the source of all the "waiting" in your logs): ① at the window end the swing leg is not yet attached — clock stops, whole body frozen waiting for it; ② interlock at the window start: no lift unless the other 5 feet are all ATTACHED; ③ threshold at the window start: some stance cup still shallower than −50 kPa (a freshly attached neighbor is a "soft shoulder"), wait for the pump to pull it deeper, alarm after 15 s; ④ any stance foot leaking — clock paused during the 2 s rescue window, freeze on timeout. This "clock that can stop" is the essential difference between the climb engine and the purely open-loop P3 gait.

4. The math of stance: 0.3 millimeters per frame

How does a velocity command become stance-foot targets? Three steps (gait.py):

① Stride vector u = velocity × stance duration        (climbing: T_stance = 3.6 s × 5/6 = 3 s)
   e.g. vx=13 mm/s straight → u ≈ 40 mm (exactly the climbing single-step cap climb_max_step)

② When turning, u differs per leg: u = (v + ω×r) × T_stance
   — rigid-body velocity field: legs far from the turning axis travel longer arcs; turning in place, the six u directions all differ

③ Within stance, the target slides at constant speed from stance position + u/2 to stance position − u/2:
   target = stance position + u × (0.5 − phase progress)

In the 50 Hz loop, a speed of 15 mm/s means the target moves only 0.3 mm per frame. The servos receive a series of position commands spaced very closely, which looks like continuous motion. To a servo, "walking" is just a stream of tiny pulse-width tweaks: 1500 µs → 1499.7 µs → 1499.4 µs…

5. Inverse kinematics: from "where the foot should be" to three joint angles

Now every leg has a target point (body frame). Turning it into angles takes two steps (robot.py → kinematics.py):

Step one: change into the leg's own frame

Body frame (X,Y,Z)
  → subtract this leg's hip-axis mounting position (mount_x, mount_y)     # e.g. L2 mounted at (0, 81.5)
  → undo this leg's neutral orientation angle mount_angle                # e.g. L2 rotate −90°
  → leg frame (x,y,z): x along the leg's neutral direction outward, z up

(If the body also has a pose trim while climbing, body_shift / pitch / roll, an inverse transform is applied first, in this same step.)

Step two: three angles, two geometry problems

γ = atan2(y, x)                       # top-view bearing, coxa points straight at the target
r = √(x²+y²) − 43                     # subtract the coxa flat segment, leaving a two-link problem
d = √(r²+z²)                          # straight-line distance from the hip (femur axis) to the foot

θ = acos( (81² + 123.7² − d²) / (2·81·123.7) )      # apex angle of the triangle = knee angle
α = atan2(z,r) + acos( (81² + d² − 123.7²) / (2·81·d) )  # line-of-sight angle + elevation
Where WorkspaceError comes from: for the triangle to exist, 42.7 mm < d < 204.7 mm (difference and sum of the two bar lengths). Target too far / too near and the acos in the law of cosines has no solution — the code raises WorkspaceError, and the climbing script catches it and freezes the whole robot in place (one of the two parachutes). All the "3 mm envelope margin" and "compensation tail budget" accounting in climb.py exists to guarantee that stance targets, dragged ever further by the body motion, never reach the d=204.7 wall.

The knee has two mirror solutions, "knee in / knee out"; the formula fixes one branch (your assembly direction); IK is recomputed independently every frame, and the same target always gives the same angles, so it never "jumps solutions".

6. Calibration table: from joint angle to pulse width µs

Servos do not know "degrees". They only know pulse width: a 500–2500 µs high pulse every 20 ms; the width of the pulse sets the horn angle, and the internal potentiometer closes the loop. So the last step is looking up each servo's calibration (18 ServoCal entries in config.py, all measured and fitted on 2026-08-08):

servo_deg = sign × (joint angle − attach_deg)          # first convert to "the servo's own degrees"
us = midpoint near 1500 + servo_deg × (us_p45 − us_m45) / 90   # linear map, then clamp to 500–2500

Three easy points of confusion:

  • attach_deg (zero offset): the horn is splined on, and where it lands decides "servo center = how many joint degrees". The official design values are coxa −8° / femur 35° / tibia 68°; your table holds individually measured values (e.g. L2 femur 47.81°).
  • Left/right mirror: the right legs are mirror assemblies of the left, so the femur/tibia servo rotation is opposite to the joint convention — the formula does not change, just swap the two ±45° pulse widths (in the table R legs are 1045/1955, L legs 1955/1045).
  • The knee is not sent θ: the calibration table uses the bend angle k = 180° − θ (straight = 0), so the code says tibia.joint_deg_to_us(180 − θ).

7. The last hop: serial packet and PWM

With the 18 pulse widths computed, the Raspberry Pi packs them into one serial packet for the Servo2040 driver board:

On receipt the Servo2040 updates the pulse-width registers of its 18 PWM pins, and the hardware keeps outputting. Each servo gets its own pulse and its internal potentiometer + motor loop turns it to the target angle. The two 50 Hz values are a coincidence and independent: the 50 Hz control loop is the software tick, the 50 Hz PWM is the servo signal standard.

The most important pitfall of the whole chain: open loop. Servos have no position read-back — the Pi only knows "the pulse width was sent", not whether the joint actually turned there. Under load, backlash + structural compliance let the actual angle drift, and the foot ends up several millimeters off: that is the physical root of the 08-19 video, where the press reaction bent the stance leg chains 13–27 mm, and of the nominal 13 mm press_delta only pressing in a few millimeters and having to be deepened to 18. Every "measure and back-fill" and "criteria on pressure/current, not commanded position" practice in the code is a way around this blind spot. (The pneumatic side does have feedback: cup pressure sensor → −30 kPa attachment criterion → only then dare to lift the next leg. Motion is open loop, adhesion is closed loop, and the safety of climbing rests entirely on the latter.)

8. Wrapping up: what actually happens in one frame (20 ms)

1. Read the keyboard: velocity command (vx, vy, wz)
2. eng.update(dt, vx, vy, wz)                        # climb.py
   ├ advance the phase clock (or pause for interlock / threshold / not attached / leak)
   ├ swing leg: compute this frame's position per the state machine (VENT→…→WAIT), drive the valves
   └ stance legs: translate targets backward by v·dt (≈0.3 mm), including slip compensation
   → return 6 foot targets (body frame)
3. bot.move_feet(targets)                            # robot.py
   ├ per leg: body frame → leg frame (subtract mount position, rotate by neutral angle)
   ├ per leg: leg_ik → γ, α, θ                       # kinematics.py
   ├ per joint: calibration table joint_deg_to_us → pulse width   # config.py
   └ 18 pulse widths ordered by channel number
4. driver.set_all_pulses_us(...)                     # driver.py
   → 0xD3 serial packet → Servo2040 → 18-channel PWM → servos turn
5. Another thread/tick: pneumatic state machine ctl.update(dt) runs valves and pump, reads cup pressures
6. sleep until the next 20 ms
