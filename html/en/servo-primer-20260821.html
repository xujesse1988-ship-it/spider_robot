Servo primer

This machine has 18 servos: the most numerous, most expensive and most misunderstood part on the whole robot. A servo is not a "motor" — you do not give it a voltage, you give it an angle, and it closes the loop internally to get there. But that loop is sealed inside the case; the outside never sees the position. The program only ever knows "the command was sent", never whether the joint actually got there. More than half of the pitfalls hit while climbing grow out of that one sentence.

1What is inside the case

A standard servo = small DC motor + reduction gear train + position potentiometer + a control board, packed into a small box, with three wires out: power, ground, signal. The control board keeps comparing "the angle the signal wire asks for" with "the actual angle the potentiometer measures", drives the motor toward whichever side the error points, and stops when the error is about zero — a complete position loop, just inside the box.

Orange is the command flow, blue dashed is the feedback flow. The feedback never leaves the case — this one figure explains half of the design decisions later in this project.

How it differs from other "things that turn"

TypeWhat you give itWhat it gives youGood for

This project uses the third kind. Servos themselves split into several families (analog / digital / bus / joint module, plus three motion types: limited, continuous rotation, multi-turn), expanded in Section 3; what switching to another type would mean for this machine is costed in Section 10.

2How it understands an "angle"

The signal wire carries a square wave: one high pulse every 20 milliseconds; the width of the pulse sets where the horn stops. By industry convention 1500 µs is center and 1000/2000 µs are the usual ends; this project widens the range to 500–2500µs to use the full travel, with a measured slope of about 10 µs = 1°. The 20 ms gap between pulses does not mean "released" — as long as pulses keep coming, the servo keeps pushing against that angle.

The joint-angle field uses the measured calibration of the L2 femur servo (channel 10): attach = 47.81° (at center the joint is actually at 47.8°, not 0°), servo's own ∓45° = 1952.5 / 1047.5 µs. On this channel a larger pulse width gives a smaller joint angle — left and right legs are mirror-assembled; the direction difference does not change the formula, it is absorbed into the two endpoints of the calibration table. Swap in a different servo and the same pulse width maps to a completely different joint angle, which is why all 18 channels must be calibrated individually. Also, the slider's 500–2500 µs is a software soft limit; the real mechanical travel is narrower, and going beyond it hits structure — the code has a separate joint-angle envelope check for that.

Digital vs. analog servos

The difference is not in the signal (both take the same pulse width) but inside: an analog servo drives the motor at roughly the 50 Hz rhythm, with a large dead band, jitter and weak holding force; a digital servo drives at several hundred Hz internally, with a small dead band, fast response and clearly higher holding torque, at the cost of more current and heat. Joints must use digital servos — on the wall every stance leg carries shear load continuously, and that holding torque is what it relies on.

3The main families of servos

First, two things both called "servo"

In Chinese "伺服电机" (servo motor) and "舵机" (RC servo) are often mixed up, but a search leads to two different worlds:

  • RC servo (the kind this page is about): motor, gears, feedback and driver all in one small box, three wires out, tens to a few hundred yuan.
  • Industrial servo: brushless motor + encoder + a separate drive, with the loop closed in the drive; the interface is pulse train, EtherCAT or CANopen; thousands per set, and the size and weight are beyond what a DIY robot can carry.

When searching use "舵机" or RC servo, not "伺服电机" — that leads into a completely unrelated industry.

With the first two generations you can only "send the command and pray"; real feedback starts with the third. This divide determines what the control software can look like — four of the five pitfalls in Section 6 are stuck on this line.

Models change fast, so remembering the category is more useful than remembering models. Some common ones as search entry points: analog: the SG90, MG996R family; digital: DS3218 / DS3235 / ZOSKAY, high-end RC is Savox, Futaba; bus servos: Feetech SCS / STS series, Hiwonder LX-16A, and the most complete but priciest ecosystem, Robotis Dynamixel; joint modules: Unitree GO-M8010, DaMiao DM-J4310, MyActuator RMD-X, the ones used in quadruped robot dogs.

By motion type: three kinds

By construction: the four parts that decide life and price

A climbing leg is exactly side load + long periods near stall, the two harshest servo conditions stacked together, so all-metal gears and double bearings are hard requirements in this project, not bonuses.

By size: three tiers

  • Micro (9 g class): SG90 types, a few yuan each, plastic gears, 1–2 kg·cm. Enough for camera gimbals and toys.
  • Standard (about 40×20×40, 55–65 g): this project's tier, 15–40 kg·cm, the workhorse size for robot joints, and the whole ecosystem of structural parts is built around it.
  • High torque (1/5-scale car): 60 kg·cm and up, 100–200 g each. Strong enough, but the weight is fatal for wall climbing.

Do not buy the cheapest no-name. Near-stall is the normal state while climbing; the consequence of stripped gears is not "this leg is weak", it is the whole robot falling off the wall. At the same price, buy two fewer spares and a brand with a reputation.

5Is the torque enough: only the current can tell

A servo does not tell you how hard it is working, but the current does. It is the only window in this project through which force can be "seen", so the driver board's current reading is shown in the status line from start to finish.

measured in this project

Single-leg press peak 0.73 A; holding a hanging weight after attachment, 0.61 A continuous.

Whole robot walking 2–4 A continuous, ~12 A transient — this number is why the servo master switch must be a ≥15 A relay.

Five-foot support on the wall carries shear load continuously, i.e. a continuous 3 A-class current + continuous heating. This is normal, not an anomaly.

Three criteria built on current

  • Total current must fall back to that pose's baseline after attachment. If it does not = that leg's preload has not been fully absorbed and the servo is fighting the vacuum — wasted power, wasted heat.
  • Current climbing cycle by cycle without falling back = internal stress accumulating. On 08-20 the whole robot measured a climb from 0.73 A to about 1.9 A, the electrical evidence of the "elastic ratchet".
  • Current jumping during press before the expected depth is reached = it hit something; stop immediately.

6Servos misbehave: five behaviors under load

None of the following five appears in any datasheet, yet each has caused real consequences in this project. Their common root is the figure in Section 1: you send a position, you get "roughly that position", and you have no way of knowing the difference.

One · Give: under load it does not stop at the commanded position

The motor's holding torque is finite; the larger the external force, the further the actual angle from the command — like a very stiff spring, not a rigid body. The most expensive appearance of this in the project: the foot preload travel is 13 mm by geometry, but on the real machine only a few mm went in, most of it eaten by "servo position error under load + backlash"; the default ended up at press_delta_mm = 18, trading command overshoot for real compression.

Two · Backlash: a little free play on reversal

A metal gear train always has clearance. For the same target angle, arriving from above and from below stops at different positions. This makes "return to the original position" inherently imprecise.

Three · Torque saturation: stored energy has a ceiling

Measured elastic rebound when lifting a single leg caps at about 21 mm; lifting more does not add — because servo torque tops out and no more energy can be stored. Bad news (the leg chain is being bent all the time) and good news (the ratchet effect has a ceiling, the machine does not slide down without bound).

Four · It is a spring and stores energy

Servo give + rubber deformation of the cup = one spring in series. Before a leg lifts, this spring keeps storing energy, released all at once the instant the cup's seal ruptures — measured 83% of the whole-robot slip happens in those 1–2 frames. The fix is not a stiffer servo but handing this leg's load to the other five before venting, so there is no energy left in the spring.

Five · Heat and derating

A servo holding load continuously gets hot. A rough way to judge whether it has left the safe zone: after adding 3 mm of preload, if the current increase is still gentle, no jump, no odd noise, not too hot to touch, then there is margin; a jump means it is already near the stall region.

One iron rule: never force a joint by hand while enabled. 35 kg·cm of stall torque is enough to break gears, and the angle you force is fake — let go and it turns itself back to the command. To adjust a pose, cut servo power first.

7Assembly and calibration: misalignment is normal

The horn is splined onto the output shaft. This project's servos have a 25-tooth spline, i.e. 360 ÷ 25 = 14.4° per step — the horn can only sit at these discrete angles, in the worst case ±7.2° off. So the official mounting angles (−8° / 35° / 68°) are design intent, not tolerance requirements; misalignment is normal, no need to take it apart and remount.

The right way is three steps:

  1. Center first, then mount the horn: power up with all servos at 1500 µs, and with power on push the horn onto the nearest tooth (servo_center.py).
  2. Measure two points: swing the joint to −45° and +45° and note the corresponding pulse widths.
  3. Write the residual into the calibration table: attach_deg records "what the joint actually is at servo center"; if the direction is reversed, change sign. One entry per servo, 18 in all.
how much work this is

Calibrating 18 channels is the most time-consuming step of the whole assembly stage; this project spent several days and wrote a dedicated least-squares fit script (calib_fit.py): 2–4 points per servo, fit slope and zero, judge by residual. The full algorithm of the calibration table and a reproducible worked example are in the "From walking commands to 18 servos" illustrated page.

A failure worth knowing: a stripped horn

The symptom is "this joint's center angle changed by itself". The cause is the horn's spline bore worn round and slipping on the output shaft. Replace the horn, but that channel must then be recalibrated — attach_deg is no longer the old value.

8Power, power-up order and power-loss behavior

Never share a power rail with the controller

18 servos moving together can pull 12 A transient, and the voltage dips visibly. If the Raspberry Pi and the servos share one supply, the Pi reboots at the instant a servo stalls — a reboot on the wall means a fall. This project: the 2S battery feeds the servos directly, a separate 5V/5A buck feeds the Pi, and the two share only ground.

Power-up order: pulse widths first, then enable

At the instant of power-up a servo slams straight to the position the signal wire asks for. So the correct order is set all 18 target pulse widths first, then close servo power — the firmware guarantees the enable instant lands directly in place, no wild jump. Whole-robot power-up has one more step: send the crouch pose first (closest to the unpowered lying pose, smallest jump), then transition slowly to standing.

Power loss = collapse; a feature, not a fault

Without power a servo has no holding force and the robot slumps into a heap. This project uses that as the emergency-stop mechanism: servo master power goes through a relay controlled by the Pi; on normal script exit, a crash, or even a killed process, the GPIO is released, the relay opens, and the servos lose power immediately.

But on the wall "collapse" is not enough. So the adhesion circuit is built to hold vacuum on power loss: with servo power off the robot goes soft, but the six cups stay stuck to the wall. The two power-loss behaviors are designed separately — the most critical item in the whole-robot safety case.

The software also sets two voltage lines: 6.4 V warning, 6.0 V raise an exception and stop. LiPo undervoltage means a weak pump and weak servos; on the wall it is the prelude to a fall.

9Three checklists

Three checks on arrival

  • Label voltage includes 7.4 V (not the 6.8 V-max version)
  • Control angle is 180°, not 270°
  • Metal gears, horn and screws included
  • Power each one individually and sweep 1000→2000 µs; listen for odd noises, watch for sticking

Before assembly

  • Center to 1500 µs before mounting the horn, keep power on
  • Push the horn onto the nearest tooth, leave the residual to calibration
  • Left/right legs mirror-assembled — the direction difference goes into the calibration table, not the formula
  • Measure ±45° right after assembly and back-fill

Before every run

  • Pulse widths first, then enable; prop the body up first
  • Check voltage: do not start below 6.4 V
  • Watch the current in the status line: it must fall back after attachment
  • Keep hands out of the joint travel while enabled

10What if we switched to another type

Put the types of Section 3 back onto this machine, and first lay down one hard constraint nobody gets around:

weight screens first

The whole-robot hard weight limit is 2.5 kg, and the 18 servos are the largest single block of weight — one leg (3 servos + printed parts) measures 260 g, six legs is 1.56 kg, 80% of the whole-robot budget without adhesion.

So the path "swap in a stronger actuator" loses half its options to weight first; only then do money and interface get a say.

Correcting an earlier sentence on this page. It used to say bus servos are "2–4× the price" — that was comparing against the Dynamixel tier. Domestic bus servos at the same torque tier are only slightly pricier, and the difference over 18 units is less scary than imagined. The real cost is not money but redoing the driver layer and the harness: serial protocol, daisy-chain power, ID assignment, dropout handling all have to be built.

conclusion

Building the machine first with PWM digital servos is still the right choice — cheap, mature ecosystem, replaceable at any time, and this machine's structural parts, driver board and the whole open-source design are built around them.

But be clear about what you bought: a cheap, strong actuator that reports absolutely nothing. Every "measure and back-fill" and "criteria on pressure and current, not on commanded position" practice on this page is a patch over that blind spot. When force control or finer gaits are needed, the first upgrade to consider is bus servos, not FOC modules.
