Wall-climbing hexapod knowledge map

What really eats the time on this machine is not kinematics and gaits — those two chapters are textbook material, a week of reading, and the code is 52 lines plus 102 lines. What decides success are four much humbler things: getting the air seal to hold, getting the power stable, measuring the geometry right, and designing the experiments right. Below, each is taken apart by "where in the project it was used", followed by a learning order and an honest difficulty verdict.

A · The knowledge landscape: thirteen areas

Four groups. The first line of each card is the "anchor in the project" — the file or number where this knowledge lands; the mark in the corner means: prerequisite cannot start without it, learn by doing the project itself teaches it, copy existing a mature solution can be taken as is.

I · Mechanics and motion making it move

Serial-mechanism kinematics

learn by doing

All the math in kinematics.py is 52 lines: atan2 for the coxa sweep, the law of cosines for the knee, reachability |l2−l3| < d < l2+l3.

  • Forward and inverse kinematics: trigonometry, law of cosines, planar two-link solution; being able to derive it once by hand is enough
  • Coordinate transforms: body frame → leg frame is a translation plus a rotation about Z; body pose uses an RPY inverse transform (robot.py)
  • Workspace and envelope margin: keep a safety distance from the reachability boundary (3 mm in this project), or the stance phase runs out of solutions halfway
  • The real difficulty is coupling: on a 3-DOF leg the foot position and cup orientation are locked together — pick the foothold and the cup's direction is fixed, with no fourth ankle joint to compensate

Gait and motion planning

learn by doing

gait.py gives three phase tables (tripod/wave/climb) in 102 lines; the climbing engine climb.py is 1019 lines — the difference is all "coupling with adhesion".

  • Phase-based gait: duty, per-leg phase offset, constant-speed backward sweep in stance, smoothstep plus sine lift in swing
  • Static stability: support polygon and center-of-mass projection; on the wall, five-foot support moving one foot at a time
  • Rigid-body velocity field v + ω × r: this is the formula behind each leg having a different stride when turning
  • Event driven: the phase clock can be stopped by adhesion events — from "walk by the timetable" to "wait for physical confirmation", the biggest software change from floor to wall
  • Lift order is also design: three consecutive same-side lifts piled the disturbance on one side; changed to diagonal alternation R3→L1→R2→L3→R1→L2

Mechanical design and 3D printing

prerequisite

tools/generate_climbing_parts.py uses trimesh + manifold3d booleans to carve a tibia cavity as a "full-shape negative" of the purchased cup's three-view drawing.

  • Parametric modeling: union/difference/intersection, solids of revolution, extrusion; dimensions live in PARAMS, change a number and regenerate
  • Print orientation decides feasibility: the suction tibia can only be printed upright — the square shaft is 4.5 mm from the flat back while the cavity radius is 17 mm; lying flat it would cut through the bed
  • Tolerances and fits: printed hole diameters must be checked with calipers; part-level tricks like hex pockets against rotation and locating pins
  • Materials: PETG first for leg parts, walls and infill by load; a 25-tooth horn spline = 14.4° per step, assembly residual can only be fixed by software calibration, do not expect to mount it accurately
II · Mechanics and energy keeping it on the wall

Statics and force budget

prerequisite

One Ø30 cup @−50 kPa measured: 25 N normal pull-off, 15 N shear on vertical glass (about 60%). Five-foot support of a 2.8 kg robot: shear safety factor ≈2.7.

  • Adhesion force F = ΔP × A, and why shear is far less than normal
  • Peel moment ≈ m·g·d/h: every centimeter the center of mass moves toward the wall is real margin — so the body hugs the wall and the battery sits on the wall side
  • Servo torque: how kg·cm converts to a joint, why 35 kg·cm is derated to 50%, what stall means
  • Safety-factor thinking: count the worst-case number of feet actually carrying load, not the nominal

Elasticity, stored energy and the ratchet

prerequisite

The most expensive lesson of this project: "stepping in place" on the wall stalled for four days, finally nailed by frame-by-frame video quantification — 83% of the slip happens in the 1–2 frames when the vented seal ruptures, not slipping, not sliding.

  • Springs in series: lateral deformation of the bellows cup + the servo's give under load add up to one spring that keeps storing energy before the leg lifts
  • Ratchet mechanism: elastic give is "locked in" by the next attachment as irreversible displacement, six times per cycle, enough to eat the whole stride
  • The energy view beats the force view: slow venting was rejected by the data outright — the force release is not gradual with pressure, the seal breaks and the whole step happens at once
  • The cure is handover, not compensation: before venting, spread this leg's load over the other five (δ/5 each; the six-leg command mean is unchanged = the body does not move), then vent at force ≈ 0
  • Be able to write a ten-line 1-D spring model: Appendix A of HANDOVER-DESIGN.md reproduces the measured 26% net advance — modeling need not be complex, it needs the right mechanism

Weight budget

learn by doing

2.5 kg is the hard cap; minus 1.9 kg for the robot without adhesion, only 0.6 kg is left for the whole pneumatic system, against a rough estimate of 0.94–1.11 kg. The budget was over from day one.

  • Weigh every part on arrival and log it; the trend matters more than any single point (docs/en/weight-log.md)
  • Weight saving is a prioritized engineering action: first flight with one pump (−130 g), the small tank, hollowed printed parts
  • Sensitivity to weight in wall climbing is on a different scale from floor robots — overweight eats the adhesion margin directly
III · Air and electricity making it reliable

Vacuum and pneumatics

prerequisite

Pump → check valve → vacuum tank → manifold → six normally-closed 3-way valves → six cups, plus a check valve per foot for fault isolation.

  • Basic quantities of negative pressure: kPa gauge, pump limit (555 dual-head about −75 to −85), pump-down time constant ≈ volume/flow (first-order exponential, same shape as RC)
  • Component vocabulary: the P/A/R ports of a 3/2-way valve, normally open/closed, check-valve direction, why a tank speeds up response
  • Vacuum hold on power loss is the lifeline of this design: wire it so that on power loss the cup stays connected to the stopped pump, and the diaphragm pump's internal check valves lock the vacuum — the robot does not drop on power loss, and the valves draw zero power while attached at rest
  • Leak hunting: every joint is a leak point; soapy water plus binary search by pinching tubes segment by segment; hysteresis control (−60/−75 kPa) reduces pump cycling
  • Dirt lies to you: dust blocking a pressure tap seals in a fake vacuum and the state machine thinks it is attached — hence a filter on every foot

Electronics and power integrity

prerequisite

The class of problem most able to "kill a whole day" in this project: a red-LED no-boot suspected as a fried board, a morning of troubleshooting, fixed by swapping the charger.

  • Power architecture: 2S direct to the servos / separate 5 V 5 A for the Pi (never a shared rail; a stalled-servo dip reboots the Pi) / boost to 12 V for pump and valves
  • Switching devices: MOSFET board, optocoupler isolation, flyback diodes for inductive loads; the relay must switch the positive high side — ground is shared with the Pi through USB, so a low-side switch would be bypassed
  • Analog chain: 4.5 V sensors divided into a 3.3 V system, ADS1115 single-ended channels, PGA range, polling the conversion-ready bit, two-point calibration giving kPa = 25 × (V − V_atm)
  • Power integrity: undervoltage history flags, transient margin and USB port choice can all reboot the system mid-air; check the power first, suspect the logic later
  • Reading noise: on reading dropouts, take a spectrum first — a small mains-frequency share means electrostatic coupling, not a bad contact
IV · Software and method making it inspectable and trustworthy

Embedded real-time software

learn by doing

The 50 Hz main loop must fit 18 servo commands + 7 I2C pressure reads; without rate limiting it jitters periodically — the read cache and burst limiting in adhesion.py exist for this.

  • Protocol implementation: match the serial protocol byte by byte against the firmware source, 14-bit values split into two 7-bit bytes; header checks, no split writes
  • Time budget: sensor readings are at worst 0.11 s stale, so a criterion's confirmation window must be clearly longer than that, or the window cannot span two real samples
  • State machines and interlocks: six adhesion states, ten leg phases, the other five feet must all be attached before a lift
  • Glitch rejection: criteria use "condition holds continuously for a duration", not "N consecutive samples" — independent of sample rate
  • Hardware abstraction + mocks: the full logic and all 81 tests run without hardware; this is what makes remote development possible
  • Black-box logging: line buffering plus periodic fsync; a failed write only warns and never crashes the control loop — both later quantified reports depended on it

The control view (this project has no PID)

learn by doing

18 PWM servos: no position read-back, no torque mode, no per-channel enable. The only thing you can do is send position commands.

  • Changing the command = changing the force: an attached foot cannot slip, so an increment in position command actually redistributes load — the whole principle behind the "zero-force handover"
  • Current is an indirect observation of force: if total current does not fall back after attachment, the servos are fighting the vacuum; a cycle-by-cycle climb is internal stress accumulating
  • Quasi-static: handover and lean are applied at a constant 10 mm/s, slow enough to ignore dynamics
  • When not to close the loop: when neither sensor precision nor actuator bandwidth supports it, open loop plus interlocks plus timeout freeze is more reliable than a forced feedback loop

Measurement and calibration

prerequisite

The most laborious job of the whole project: pulse width ↔ joint angle measured and fitted for each of 18 servos, plus a hard-fought battle over "how long is the tibia" (L3-DISPUTE-OPEN.md).

  • Least-squares fitting: a servo is a linear model; measure 2–4 points, fit slope and zero, judge by residual
  • Measurement design: height-difference method (sinα = Δh/l), three-side method (law of cosines), projection method — pick one that is reproducible and immune to lateral offset
  • Error propagation: near vertical the sine flattens and a 1 mm reading error becomes 4.5° — so the calibration pose must be chosen, not arbitrary
  • Defining the reference matters more than measuring: where the knee axis is, where the foot is, which line is the cup axis — all three were once defined wrong, and all three were corrected only by cross-examining three-view drawings
  • State-dependent geometry: the cup is 19 mm tall free, 7.5 mm pressed to seal, 8 mm under vacuum — hence "press to seal first, then pump"; pumping barely moves it

Experiment design and data analysis

prerequisite

logs_analysis/ab_quant.py: frame-by-frame NCC template matching + parabolic sub-pixel + static-reference camera-drift correction, splitting each round's 74 mm of slip by event.

  • Quantify before tuning: without this frame-by-frame quantification, "83% at the seal-rupture instant" would never have been found, only endless blind tuning of compensation coefficients
  • Measuring displacement from video: template matching, sub-pixel interpolation, mm/px calibration, static reference to remove camera drift
  • A/B discipline: re-run the baseline the same day, tripod and zoom locked, sync marker before the first round, always judge on round 2 (round 1 is the transient ramp from zero)
  • Dare to let data reject your own idea: slow venting, an idea that "sounded reasonable", was shot down on the spot by the profile data

Systems engineering and working discipline

prerequisite

The roadmap puts the most expensive assumption first: the single-leg wall decision gate costs only the first batch, ¥600–950; if it fails, stop the loss and the 15 other servos are never bought.

  • Risk first: verify the one thing most likely to overturn the whole plan, not the part you are most sure of
  • Acceptance criteria must be quantifiable: 50 cycles >95%, 1.5 kg hanging for 5 minutes, slip per round <10 mm — not "feels fine"
  • Degradation and fail-safe: rescue a leak first, freeze and alarm on timeout; when the process dies the GPIO is released, the relay opens, the normally-closed valves hold vacuum
  • Write the retracted conclusions into the docs too: this project has a dedicated section "wrong conclusions produced this round, do not reuse" — so you do not pick the wrong one up again weeks later
  • Safety discipline: safety rope on at all times, padding on the floor, nobody under a hanging-weight test, never leave a charging LiPo unattended

C · Learning path: catch up stage by stage

This is a real sequence — each step is the prerequisite of the next, and each has a "stop if it fails" criterion. Learned in this order, the knowledge is pulled out by problems, not memorized in advance.

P0

Get one leg moving

To learn: 3D printing and assembly, the concept of servo pulse width, SSH/serial on the Pi, Python packages and tests. Get the simulation running first; this can start before the hardware arrives.

Criterion: three servos can be swung by the program; simulation and tests all green.

P1

Vacuum circuit on the rig

To learn: pneumatic components, GPIO and MOSFET, ADC and dividers, two-point calibration, leak hunting. The physical foundation of the whole project is set here — the three curves (pump-down time, leak rate, release time) and the tilt tolerance are all measured here.

Criterion: one foot holds 1.5 kg on vertical glass for 10 minutes; one attach-confirm-release cycle <2 s.

P2

Single leg on the wall — decision gate

To learn: single-leg IK integrated with the adhesion state machine, measured check of the force budget, load-bearing fixture design. The only stop-loss point of the whole project; do not buy the second batch before passing.

Criterion: 50 attach/release cycles >95% success; 1.5 kg hanging for 5 minutes + 1 kg side pull without detaching.

P3

Whole-robot assembly and floor walking

To learn: the methodology of 18-channel calibration (the most laborious job of the project, several days), gait parameters, drift compensation. By here the "robotics" part is done.

Criterion: walk 2 m straight, turn 360° in place, single-leg point-to-point error <5 mm.

P4

Whole robot on the wall

To learn: converting the gait to event-driven, real-time budget for multi-channel sensing, fail-safe and degradation, black-box logging, and — the mechanics problems that only show up on the wall. This project was stuck here for four days and found the root cause only through video quantification.

Criterion: climb 1 m on vertical glass, traverse 0.5 m, hover 5 minutes after power loss without detaching.

P5

Payload, floor-to-wall transition, perception

To learn: transition sequencing and center-of-mass management, vision and attitude sensing. Kinematically verified feasible (with the body level, wall distance to the front hip 80–200 mm all have valid footholds); the difficulty is the sequencing.

Criterion: ordered as needed, no single gate.

D · Six things the books will not tell you

These were hit by this project itself. Each cost at least a full day, several cost four.

One · On a 3-DOF leg, position and orientation are locked together

For the cup to seal, its axis must be roughly perpendicular to the wall; but the leg has only three joints, so once the foothold is chosen the cup direction is fixed, with no fourth degree of freedom to compensate. As a result, stand height 90 mm, stance reach 176 mm, stride ≤40 mm are not comfortable tuned values but hard constraints solved backward from "bellows cup ±15° tolerance". The general floor pose reach=130 moved onto the wall makes the cup meet the surface at 16.7° and simply not seal.

Two · A "normally closed valve" label cannot be trusted; vacuum hold on power loss has to be assembled from the wiring

The 3-way valves bought vent the outlet to air when unpowered — wired by the label, the whole robot drops at the first power loss. The actual fix is reversing the tubing: when unpowered the cup stays connected to the stopped diaphragm pump, whose internal check valves lock the vacuum. A free bonus comes with it: while attached at rest the coils are unpowered, six valves at zero power. A component's real behavior must be tested by blowing through it yourself; do not trust the listing.

Three · The elastic energy of a lifted leg drags the whole robot down at the instant the seal ruptures

On flat ground the robot can walk forward, but on the glass wall it seems to march in place all the time… every time it lifts a leg it gets dragged down a bit. project log · the day of the first wall climb
If the lifted leg's elastic energy is large, lifting it releases that energy and the whole body moves down a distance instead; I found that this is what keeps it from advancing. project log · root cause spotted four days later, confirmed by data the next day

Frame-by-frame quantification: during the 0.4 s of venting after the valve opens the body moves <0.15 mm; after the seal ruptures the drop completes in 1–2 frames (<100 ms), single-leg bounces from 5.8 to 20.9 mm (a fourfold spread between legs), and total current ratchets up every cycle without falling back. The cure is not slow venting (rejected by the data) but handing the load over before venting.

Four · Position-controlled actuators can still redistribute force

An attached foot cannot slip, so the position command you give it actually changes force. The leg to be lifted has its command retreat "uphill" by δ to unload itself, and the other five each take δ/5 "downhill" — the vector sum of the six commands is zero, the body command does not move, but that leg's force drops to near zero and there is no energy to release when the seal breaks. This idea holds even on servos with no torque interface; it is the most elegant step of the project.

Five · Power problems disguise themselves as everything else

Raspberry Pi red LED, no boot, still red with every connector pulled, multimeter reading 0 V — a whole morning checking the MOSFET board channel by channel for breakdown, fixed in the end by swapping a phone charger; the board was never broken. The same week: the servo board reported undervoltage on the blue USB 3.0 port and was fine on the black USB 2.0 port. The order should be reversed: swap the power supply first, look at the undervoltage flag first, suspect logic and parts later.

Six · Geometric truth is not measured, it is defined first and then measured

The question "how long is the tibia" was argued for a whole day, and the root was not measurement precision but three undefined reference points: where the knee axis is, where the foot ends, which line is the cup axis. It converged only by drawing three views and cross-examining them (tibia_len = 123.7; the official value is 134). And the cup axis is not "the line from knee to lip center" at all; the two differ by 25.2° — an error that at one point made the whole "stand perpendicular" implementation wrong. Cross-examining drawings is the most effective way to resolve a disagreement, far cheaper than arguing on.

E · Cost, time and cut-down routes

How far to goParts costKnowledge neededWhat you get to play with
Single-leg adhesion rig (P0–P2) ¥600~950 Pneumatics + power + ADC + single-leg IK + state machine Verify wall-climbing feasibility, film "one foot holding 3 kg"
Floor hexapod (skip the pneumatics) ¥1300~2200 Only groups I and IV: kinematics, gait, calibration, embedded A complete walking robot, with a mature community path
Whole-robot wall climbing (full P0–P4) ¥2000~2800 All thirteen areas, plus experiment quantification skills Climbing on glass/tile walls + hovering after power loss

In time, this project went from single-leg acceptance to the first wall climb and on to locating and solving "stepping in place" in one month of intensive development (166 commits). Less than a week of that went into kinematics and gait; the rest went into calibration, pneumatics, power and quantified experiments.

Cut-down advice: if you only want to learn robotics, a floor hexapod is enough and saves the whole of group III and half the money; if you want to play with climbing without building the whole robot, a single-leg rig for a few hundred yuan meets all the most interesting physics (sealing, peeling, elasticity). Cut-downs not recommended: dropping the pressure sensors for timed valves — without pressure feedback the state machine cannot tell "attached or not", and going on the wall becomes luck; dropping the black-box log — after an incident nothing can be traced.

F · An honest verdict

The good news

The part that looks most "high-barrier" — kinematics, gait, robot control — is exactly the most standardized and easiest to copy. The leg inverse solution is 52 lines, the gait engine 102, the structure has a complete MIT-licensed open-source design, the servo board has ready-made firmware. Someone who writes Python and knows trigonometry can get a hexapod walking on the floor within two weeks.

The bad news

What decides whether this machine gets onto the wall is all "un-robotics-like": a fitting not pushed fully home and the robot drops; a bit less power margin and it reboots in mid-air; a wrong calibration reference and "stand perpendicular" comes out tilted; nobody thinks of the cup's elastic energy storage and four days of tuning go nowhere. There is no textbook for these; only measurement, records and cross-examination.

If you remember one thing

The three real breakthroughs of this project — correcting the tibia length, pinning down the undervoltage pattern, the elastic-energy root cause — came not from smarter analysis but from using physical photos, multimeter readings and frame-by-frame video to push back a plausible-sounding wrong assumption. Whether you are willing to do that decides whether you finish it, more than whether you can solve inverse kinematics.

self-check first If you cannot bring yourself to do either "read off a multimeter point by point" or "measure with calipers and cross-examine three-view drawings", this machine will most likely stop halfway — not because you cannot, but because you do not check.
